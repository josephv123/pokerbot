Introduction and Theoretical ParadigmsThe development of an autonomous agent capable of navigating the complexities of imperfect information games remains one of the preeminent challenges in computational game theory and artificial intelligence. While full-scale No-Limit Texas Hold 'Em involves a game tree complexity exceeding $10^{160}$ decision points, the simplified  continuous signal game—often referred to as "The AKQ Game" in its discrete form or "continuous poker" in its infinite form—serves as the foundational bedrock for understanding optimal frequency distributions, range balancing, and indifference principles.1This report provides an exhaustive implementation plan for a poker bot designed to compete in a simplified  Texas Hold 'Em environment, specifically tailored to the constraints and competitive landscape of the "Psychology 213" academic competition.4 The environment is characterized by alternating actions, variable or pot-limit betting structures, and a field of opponents ranging from static heuristic bots (e.g., "John1") to advanced GTO-approximators (e.g., "John3").5The strategic framework proposed herein is bipartite. First, it establishes a rigorous Game Theory Optimal (GTO) baseline derived from the von Neumann and Chen-Ankenman models, ensuring unexploitability against optimal adversaries.6 Second, it layers a sophisticated Bayesian opponent modeling system designed to identify and exploit statistical deviations in sub-optimal opponents, maximizing Expected Value (EV) beyond the zero-sum equilibrium.81.1 Historical Context: Borel vs. Von Neumann ModelsTo implement the "ULTIMATE" solution, one must first understand the lineage of the mathematical models governing the game. The earliest analysis of simplified poker was conducted by Émile Borel, who posited a model where players could only bet or fold. However, this model lacked the critical "check" mechanic found in modern poker, leading to solutions that did not accurately reflect the strategic realities of the game.2John von Neumann, in his seminal work Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (co-authored with Morgenstern), refined this by introducing the "check" option, allowing Player 1 (P1) to pass the action to Player 2 (P2) without immediately abandoning the pot. This distinction is non-trivial; it transforms the game from a simple aggressive assertion of strength into a complex interplay of "trapping" (checking strong hands) and "giving up" (checking weak hands).1The model relevant to this implementation is the "Extension 1" of von Neumann’s poker, which incorporates alternating betting.11 In this variation:Players act sequentially rather than simultaneously.P1 checks "in the dark" (or acts first).P2 can check back (ending the hand) or bet.If P2 bets, P1 can call or fold.This structure mirrors the post-flop play of Texas Hold 'Em (specifically the River street) and is the specific domain for which our bot, "Nash-Adjusted," must be optimized.61.2 The Strategic Imperative: GTO vs. Exploitative PlayA central theme in the construction of high-performance poker agents is the tension between GTO and Exploitative play. A GTO strategy is a Nash Equilibrium profile where the agent cannot lose value, regardless of the opponent's strategy, provided the agent plays perfectly. As noted in the research, if all players adopt GTO, the EV for everyone is zero (minus rake).3However, the user query implies a desire to beat players, not merely survive them. The snippet regarding "Psychology 213" suggests that opponents may be human students or imperfect bots designed by students.4 Human players are prone to "boredom," "tilt," and "work indifference," leading to predictable deviations from optimal frequencies.14 Therefore, while the core of our implementation is GTO-based to handle the formidable "John3" 5, the profit-generation engine will be strictly exploitative, leveraging the "Variable Edge Model" to punish frequency errors in "John1" and "AllIn".162. Mathematical Derivation of the Nash EquilibriumThe foundation of the bot is the rigorous calculation of the equilibrium thresholds. In a  game, the hand strength $x$ is a continuous variable $x \sim U$. Strategies are not defined by complex lookup tables for every possible floating-point value, but rather by thresholds that partition the unit interval into action regions.12.1 The General Indifference PrincipleThe core mechanism for solving these games is the Principle of Indifference. Equilibrium is found when a player’s strategy makes the opponent indifferent between their available actions with their marginal hands. For example, P1's bluffing frequency is optimal when P2 is indifferent between calling and folding with their best bluff-catcher.2Let $P$ be the size of the pot (including antes) and $B$ be the size of the bet.The game is zero-sum. Optimal Bet Sizing StrategiesWhile many versions of the  game fix the bet size (e.g., Pot Limit), sophisticated implementations allow for variable bet sizing. The choice of $B$ is a strategic variable for Player 1.4.1 The Value Function and Optimal BResearch by Ferguson and Newman demonstrates that there is an optimal bet size that maximizes the Value of the Game ($V$) for Player 1.2The value function $V(B)$ for the von Neumann game is:$$V(B) = \frac{B}{(B+1)(B+4)}$$To find the optimal bet size, we take the derivative with respect to $B$ and set to zero:$$\frac{dV}{dB} = 0 \implies B = 2$$Analysis: This is a profound result. It suggests that in a game with a pot of 1 (plus antes), the optimal bet size is exactly 2 times the ante (or the size of the pot). Betting less ($B < 2$) fails to extract maximum value from strong hands and fails to protect the bluffing range. Betting more ($B > 2$) forces P2 to fold too often, reducing the payoff from calls.2Implementation: The bot will default to $B = 2$ (Pot Sized Bet) whenever it has the option to choose the bet size, as this mathematically maximizes the edge over the opponent.4.2 Geometric Bet Sizing (Multi-Street Extension)Although the simplified game is often a single betting round, snippet 21 references "Geometric Bet Sizing." If the competition rules allow for multiple streets (e.g., Turn and River), the bot must split its betting range.Formula: $B_{street} = Pot \times (\frac{Stack}{Pot})^{1/N} - 1$This ensures that by the final street, the bot can move "All-In" smoothly, maximizing the geometric growth of the pot when holding the nuts.5.
